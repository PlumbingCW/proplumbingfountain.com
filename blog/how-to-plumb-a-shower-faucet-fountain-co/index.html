How to Plumb a Shower Faucet: What the Rough-In Involves

Plumbing a shower faucet means installing the valve body in the wall, connecting hot and cold supply lines to it, running a line up to the showerhead and, for a tub shower, a line to the spout. Everything is built in the open wall, tested and only then covered.

By the Fountain Pro Plumbing team · Published November 14, 2026

The most common mistakes are setting the valve too deep or too shallow for the finished wall, reversing hot and cold and skipping the pressure test. Each is cheap to fix before the wall is closed and expensive afterward.

This guide describes the sequence so you know what a plumber does and what to plan. In Fountain, shower plumbing usually needs a permit, and the rough-in is inspected before the walls close.

Parts of a shower plumbing setup

The pieces
PartJobNotes
Valve bodyMixes hot and cold and controls flowSet inside the wall at a fixed depth
Supply linesBring hot and cold to the valveCopper or PEX, with shutoffs nearby

The valve and trim are sold as a set. The valve body goes in at rough-in, and the trim is added after tile. That is why the brand and model must be chosen before you start.

Plan before you cut

Decide these first

  • The valve brand and type, such as pressure-balancing or thermostatic.
  • The height of the valve and the showerhead.
  • The wall thickness after tile or panels, since the valve depth depends on it.
  • Whether the supply lines are accessible from another room or the basement.
  • Whether the drain and vent are in the right place.

Use the valve's printed depth guide. Most bodies have a mark showing how far they must sit from the finished wall. Getting this right makes the trim fit and the handle turn.

Rough-in sequence

Typical order of work

  1. Shut off the water and drain the lines in the area.
  2. Frame or add backing boards where the valve and the shower arm will mount.
  3. Position the valve body at the right height and depth and fasten it to the backing.
  4. Connect hot to the left and cold to the right, as the valve's markings show.
  5. Run the riser to the showerhead drop ear and the line to the tub spout.
  6. Cap the outlets and pressure test with a gauge before closing the wall.

Good practice is to hold the pressure test for a while and watch the gauge. A drop means a leak, and finding it now is simple.

Joints and materials

Copper joints are soldered, and PEX uses crimp or expansion fittings. Each needs the right tools, and a bad joint will not show up until it is hidden inside a wall. That is the main reason many homeowners hire this out.

Whichever material you use, support the pipes properly and protect them from nail strikes. Add shutoff valves where you can reach them, so a future repair does not mean turning off the whole house.

Testing, trim and the drain

After the inspection and wall finish, install the trim kit and the showerhead. Run hot and cold separately to confirm they are connected correctly and check the diverter. A tub shower should push water up to the head when the diverter is pulled.

Do not forget the drain. A shower pan or tub needs a trap, a vent and a proper slope. If the shower drains slowly after install, a trap or vent issue is the likely cause, and drain cleaning or a vent check may be needed.

Valve types in brief

Pressure-balancing valves react to a change in pressure on the hot or cold line, such as a toilet flushing, and keep the temperature steadier. Thermostatic valves hold a set temperature more precisely. Both are common, and the trim must match the valve body.

Whichever you pick, keep the paperwork. The model number tells you which cartridge to buy years later, and it saves you from opening the wall to find out.

The anti-scald standard behind shower valves

Pressure-balancing and thermostatic shower valves are commonly made to the ASSE 1016 standard, also published as ASME A112.1016, which covers valves that guard against sudden temperature swings. Look for the marking on the valve or the box when you choose one.

When to hire a plumber

Hire a plumber if you must change the supply route, move the drain, solder copper or work in a finished wall. Also hire one if you are unsure about permits. We handle these jobs under plumbing installation, and a leaking valve or cartridge is covered under faucet repair.
